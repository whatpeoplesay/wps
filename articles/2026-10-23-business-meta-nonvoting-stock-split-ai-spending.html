Meta Plans Non-Voting Stock Split to Fund AI Spending

Meta will give every shareholder one non-voting Class C share for each share they own, a move that funds its AI buildout while keeping Mark Zuckerberg's voting control intact.

23 October 2026 | Business | By WPS Staff

Sources: Reuters, Reuters

Meta said it will create a new class of non-voting shares, giving every shareholder one Class C share for each share they own. The move works like a stock split and is meant to fund the company's huge AI buildout while keeping chief executive Mark Zuckerberg's voting control intact.

Read More

The plan is a one-time dividend: one Class C share for each outstanding Class A and Class B share, with the charter change set to take effect on January 19, 2027 and a Nasdaq listing planned. Because the new shares carry no votes, Meta can later sell them to raise money for data centers, acquisitions, or employee pay without shifting control of the company.

The announcement came with record third-quarter revenue of $64.6 billion, up 26 percent from a year earlier and above Wall Street's $63.37 billion estimate. But investors focused on the bill. Capital spending hit $34.91 billion in the quarter, and free cash flow turned negative $2.12 billion, the company's first cash burn in more than a decade. Meta also lifted the low end of its full-year spending forecast to $135 billion.

Shares fell about 5 to 7 percent on Thursday as investors weighed the AI costs against the growth. The earnings landed hours after a New Mexico judge ordered Meta to pay a $25 billion penalty in a data-privacy case linked to Cambridge Analytica, which Meta said it will appeal. At least 13 brokerages still raised their price targets on the stock, according to LSEG data.

A separate SEC filing showed the stock plan is also tied to keeping Zuckerberg in California, where a proposed wealth tax could cost him billions based on his holdings. The filing says the new share structure gives Meta room to raise growth capital while keeping its founder-led setup.

Prop 40 / The tax is the trigger; control is the mechanism. / Zuckerberg controls roughly 60% of Meta's votes while owning about 13% of its shares, because his Class B shares carry 10 votes each (Class A carries one). His salary is $1; essentially all his wealth is Meta stock. That combination is the vulnerability: any large cash need forces him to sell Class B shares, and every Class B share sold permanently reduces his grip. Per the SEC filing, selling Class B to cover a Prop 40 liability could have knocked his voting interest down to about 53%. / Class C fixes that. It's a pool of stock he can sell for cash without touching a single vote. Under the Founder Alignment Agreement signed Oct. 20, he can freely sell or transfer 20% of the Class C he receives; selling the other 80% generally requires him to simultaneously sell or convert a matching Class A or Class B share within two business days, which does trim his voting power. So the structure hands him liquidity while capping how much he can monetize without surrendering control.
