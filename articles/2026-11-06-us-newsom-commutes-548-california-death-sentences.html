Newsom commutes all 548 California death sentences to life in prison

California Governor Gavin Newsom has commuted the death sentences of all 548 people on the state's death row to life in prison without parole, in the largest mass clemency in US history.

6 November 2026 | US | By WPS Staff

Sources: USA Today, Al Jazeera

California Governor Gavin Newsom announced on Thursday, November 5, that he is commuting all 548 death sentences in the state to life in prison without the possibility of parole. In an open letter to Californians, he said state-sanctioned killing is incompatible with the state's values and undermines the foundations of the legal system. The move effectively clears California's death row, although the state has not executed a prisoner for 20 years.

The commutations do not repeal the death penalty, and they apply only to sentences already in place. About two-thirds of the cases must be reviewed by the California Supreme Court before they become final, because state law requires the court to review clemency for anyone with more than one felony conviction. Newsom imposed a moratorium on executions shortly after taking office in 2019, and California's last execution was in 2006.

Reaction to the decision split sharply. Anti-death penalty groups praised it, with Abraham Bonowitz of Death Penalty Action saying the announcement left him breathless, and the Rev. Jeff Hood calling it the height of moral leadership. The Death Penalty Information Center called it the largest mass commutation in US history. But victims' families and prosecutors condemned it. Jess Nichol, whose sister Polly Klaas was murdered, called the death penalty a distraction, while Steve Herr, whose son was murdered, called Newsom a coward and said he felt extreme anger.

The California District Attorneys Association said it strongly condemned the decision, calling it a profound disregard for the decisions of juries and judges and the rights of crime victims. Orange County District Attorney Todd Spitzer called the commutation mercy for the wolves, and Riverside County District Attorney Mike Hestrin said the governor acted without meaningful consultation with prosecutors or victims' families. Los Angeles County District Attorney Nathan Hochman said his office will keep evaluating death penalty cases until the law is changed.

Newsom's announcement came on the same day that three other states carried out executions: Florida, Indiana and Alabama each executed one man. It was only the second time this year that three executions fell on a single day. Since January 2025, 11 states have put 79 inmates to death. The Death Penalty Information Center says California has spent $6 billion on the death penalty since 1978 while carrying out just 13 executions, and a Pew Research Center poll in August found 66 percent of Americans supported the death penalty for murder, while 73 percent acknowledged the risk of executing an innocent person. Newsom's second and final term ends in January 2027, and he will be replaced by Xavier Becerra on January 4.
