Lancet report calls climate change a global health emergency

The 2026 Lancet Countdown report says climate change should be declared a global health emergency, warning that heat deaths and other health harms are accelerating faster than the world's response.

29 October 2026 | Health | By WPS Staff

Sources: Free Malaysia Today (AFP), Le Temps (AFP)

A major new report has called for climate change to be formally declared a global health emergency. The 2026 Lancet Countdown report says the health harms linked to a warming planet are growing faster than the world's response to them, and in many cases are accelerating.

Read More

The report is the tenth edition of the Lancet Countdown on health and climate change. It was led by University College London in collaboration with the World Health Organization, and it brings together 146 researchers from more than 100 institutions in more than 30 countries. It was published ahead of the COP31 climate summit in November.

The researchers found that heat was linked to an average of more than 540,000 deaths a year between 2016 and 2023, with 68 percent of those deaths attributed to climate change. In 2023 alone, heat was linked to a record 710,000 deaths, more than the deaths from malaria or HIV, with more than 70 percent linked to human-caused warming. In total, climate change caused nearly 3 million heat-related deaths between 2016 and 2023.

The report also points to wider damage. Extreme drought affected more than half of the world's land for at least one month in 2025, and heat-driven falls in worker productivity have cost the global economy more than one trillion dollars a year over the past three years. Southeast Asia and Africa were hit hardest, losing an estimated 3.9 percent and 3.2 percent of GDP, compared with 0.2 percent for Europe and 1.5 percent for the Americas.

There has been some progress. Since 2016, 56 of the World Health Organization's 194 member states have adapted their health and early-warning systems, clean energy investment reached 2.1 trillion dollars in 2025, and falling fossil fuel air pollution has averted about 323,000 deaths in at least 77 countries. But fossil fuels still supply 80 percent of global energy, and the report says progress has been too slow, too small, and is not reaching those who need it most.
