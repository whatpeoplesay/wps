Trump asks Supreme Court to set aside California ballots over citizenship claims

The Trump administration asked the Supreme Court on Monday to set aside mail ballots from about 20,000 California voters it alleges may be noncitizens.

2 November 2026 | Politics | By WPS Staff

Sources: CNN, Reuters

The Trump administration asked the Supreme Court on Monday to temporarily block California from counting mail ballots from more than 20,000 registered voters who federal officials allege are likely noncitizens. The Justice Department filed the emergency request a day before the November 3 midterm elections, asking that the disputed ballots not be counted until each voter's citizenship status can be verified.

Lower courts had already rejected the request. A federal district judge turned it down on October 28, and the 9th US Circuit Court of Appeals refused it on Friday in a 2-1 decision. The appeals court said the federal government has no power to dictate to states how to run elections, and that a state should be asked through Congress, not the courts, if different vote-by-mail procedures are wanted.

The Justice Department argued that once the challenged ballots are mixed with lawful ballots, there is no way to get them back. It said California is frustrating enforcement of a 1996 federal law that makes it a crime for noncitizens to vote in federal elections. The list of about 20,000 voters was put together by the Department of Homeland Security and sent to California officials and to Los Angeles County officials, and the department sued in federal court on October 15.

California argued that its safeguards against noncitizen voting are strong, and that the federal process for identifying noncitizens is flawed and likely full of errors. State officials said the request threatens to take away the vote of 20,000 people on vague accusations. The appeals court agreed there was a material risk that granting the request would result in citizens being disenfranchised and could jeopardise the Los Angeles County election.

The case also raised the question of timing, because the justices have long warned against federal courts changing election rules at the last minute. In September, the Supreme Court rejected an earlier attempt to restrict mail ballots, but days later it allowed the Department of Homeland Security to keep sharing citizenship data with state election officials. The emergency request put the California dispute before the court on the eve of voting, with the short time before Election Day part of what the court would have to weigh.
